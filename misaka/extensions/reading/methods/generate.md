# Generate questions

Questions do not come from nowhere. Good further questions grow out of what you have already read
in the text's construction: the work a premise does, the point where two lines diverge, a mismatch
between one passage and another. Generating questions means turning those places into questions
that can be read on.

## Where to look

1. **Load-bearing steps**: a step the argument relies on but does not argue for. Ask: what does it
   need in order to hold?
2. **Points of divergence**: where two lines of reading part. Ask: what evidence would tell them
   apart?
3. **Absences**: people, events and situations the material does not discuss. Ask: if they were
   brought in, how would the conclusion change?
4. **The material's limits**: what this material can and cannot answer. Ask: what must be read to
   answer the rest?
5. **Sliding concepts**: places where one word changes its meaning from passage to passage. Ask: is
   the slide an oversight or deliberate?

## What to write

For each question:

- **Question**: one question that can be taken straight into reading, not "this deserves further
  study".
- **Source**: which construction, passage or node it grows from.
- **Why it is worth pursuing**: what answering it would change in the understanding, not "this is
  important".
- **How to start**: which passage to read, what kind of material to look for, which existing line
  to compare it with.

## Take care

- Do not generate questions that reading cannot answer (one that needs an experiment or a survey,
  say) unless you mark them as beyond reading; such a question may suit a research run instead.
- Do not pad. Three solid questions are better than ten vague ones.
- If questions depend on one another, give their order.
- Cite existing nodes by id so the question can be taken straight back into a line of reading.
