# Change the wording, the scale or the premise

One problem can be organised on another frame: another set of terms, another scale of
observation, or one premise taken away to see what is left. The aim is not to rewrite the text but
to make its construction visible by deforming it.

## Three transformations

1. **Another wording**: restate the author's central distinction in another conceptual language
   (for example, a claim about a right restated as a claim about who bears the cost of exercising
   it). After the restatement, what that was unclear becomes clear, and what is flattened?
2. **Another scale**: enlarge the object from a person to an institution, or narrow it from a
   generation to a single conversation. When the scale changes, causes and responsibility often
   move with it. Say at which step the change happens.
3. **Another premise**: suppose one of the author's premises is false (resources are sufficient,
   institutions can be reformed, the readers are like the author) and see which part of the
   argument still holds. What still holds is where the argument's real force lies.

## What to write

- **The transformation**: say plainly what you changed: the terms, the scale or a premise.
- **The construction after it**: reorganise the object in the new terms, readably.
- **Gains and losses**: what the transformation keeps and what it drops. Do not list only the
  gains.
- **Back to the text**: which place in the text the transformation made you see again, with its
  section or pages.

## Take care

- The transformed account must still point back to specific content in the text; an analogy cut
  loose from the material is not a transformation.
- Do not use a transformation to change the subject. If you changed the question itself, say what
  you changed.
- Be especially restrained in changing a premise: first set out how the argument works when the
  premise holds, then take the premise away.
