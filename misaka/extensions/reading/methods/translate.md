# Translate across theories

Translation is not swapping a term for a synonym. Carry a statement from one theory into another
problem domain, and say **at what level the carrying holds**, and what is bound to be deformed on
the way.

## How

1. First fix the point of departure: what work does the statement do in its own theory? Which
   question does it answer, which concepts is it bound to, and what is left once those bonds are
   removed?
2. Then fix the point of arrival: which concepts does the target domain organise its experience
   with? Placed there, to which part does the statement correspond?
3. Check level by level:
   - **Concepts**: with the words changed, is the reference still the same? Where it is not
     equivalent, say how it differs.
   - **Argument**: do the original argument's steps still hold in the new setting? Which step
     relies on a premise the new setting lacks?
   - **Experience**: does the material the statement referred to have a counterpart in the new
     domain? If not, say what is missing.
4. Give the translated statement and mark its limits: under what conditions it works, and when it
   fails.

## What to write

- The statement's place and work in its original theory.
- The conceptual structure of the target domain.
- The translated statement, written in the target domain's language, not as a term-for-term swap.
- **Kept** and **lost**: what the translation preserves, what it necessarily drops, and which losses
  are acceptable.
- If the translation does not hold, say at which level it breaks; do not force out a statement.

## Take care

- Beware of "it sounds alike": similar words often carry different premises.
- Do not let one theory's authority decide a question for another.
- Whether a concept travels to the target domain is often a question that domain has argued over.
  If you know of such an argument and the materials do not contain it, say so as memory, unchecked.
- In a translation across fields (a philosophical concept applied to sociological material, say),
  be explicit about whether an empirical counterpart really exists.
- Cite places on both sides so the reader can judge for themselves whether the translation is
  credible.
