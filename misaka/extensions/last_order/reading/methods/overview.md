# Overview

Do not start by explaining a single sentence. Answer three things: what problem the author is
answering, what entitles the text to answer it this way, and where a reader who enters only here
should stand first.

## What to read

1. What kind of text it is and whom it addresses: a lecture, a letter, a treatise, a play; for
   which audience, on what occasion, as far as the material says. The kind of text decides what
   counts as its argument.
2. The title, the opening and the ending. An opening often **creates the difficulty**; an ending
   often **narrows the promise**: how much is the author finally willing to claim? Do the two
   agree?
3. The outline (`doc_outline`), to judge the construction: a narrative in time, rebuttals set side
   by side, or one construction tightened step by step? Without an outline, read the first and last
   pages and work out yourself what each group of paragraphs does.
4. A hinge: the place where the argument or the narrative changes its object, its scale or its
   tone. Give its section or pages.

## What to write

- **The author's problem**: not a topic word but the difficulty the author has to solve. Say it in
  one sentence of your own, and name whose view the author is answering where that matters.
- **Construction**: the stretches the text falls into, what each one does, and where it turns.
- **Way in**: if the reader had one hour, where to start reading and why.
- **Uncertain points**: judgements you drew from headings alone, without reading the body. Mark
  them.

## Take care

- An overview is not a summary. Retelling paragraph by paragraph is no use: give the structure and
  the place to enter.
- Do not quote a sentence you have not read. Every quotation carries its outline node or pages.
- Keep the author's wording apart from your own account: "the author says …; I take this to
  mean …".
