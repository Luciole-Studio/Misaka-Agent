# Presuppositions and absences

What does this text take as needing no argument? Whom does it leave unmentioned? What kind of world
does its answer presuppose, and if that presupposition failed, how would the conclusion change
shape?

## What to read

1. Look for the words that mark something as obvious or beyond question: "of course",
   "naturally", "everyone", "one has to", and their equivalents in the text's language.
   Presuppositions surface there, often in passing, while carrying a whole stretch of the argument.
2. Find what the text deals with and who is absent from it: whose choices does the author discuss,
   and whose are left out? Is the absence an oversight, or is it built into how the problem is set?
3. Find a situation presented as an either-or. What reason rules out a third option, and does that
   reason hold?
4. See what the author treats as the sign that the problem is solved. For whom does that sign hold?

## What to write

- **What is taken as natural**: two or three items, each with its place in the text and why you
  take it as a presupposition.
- **What it holds up**: if it were removed, which part of the argument would fall. Do not say
  vaguely that it "affects the whole text".
- **Opening and narrowing**: which questions the author's choice makes possible to ask, and which it
  makes impossible to ask.
- **The absent**: who is not there, and how that absence limits the reach of the conclusion.
- **New questions**: the questions these lines of inquiry lead to naturally and that are worth
  reading on for. Do not ask questions to fill space.

## Take care

- Questioning a presupposition is not refuting the author. Say **what work it does** instead of
  simply declaring it wrong.
- Do not offer the period's background as the explanation of everything. "That is how things were
  then" only repeats the presupposition.
- Tell apart the presuppositions the author states and those the text's structure reveals; the
  second kind is your reading and must be marked as such.
