# Reconstruct

Reconstruction is not retelling. Turn the text back into **the solving of a problem**: what the
difficulty was at the start, which concept or distinction the author brought in, what that choice
let the author do and what it forced the author to accept, and where the argument went as a result.

## What to read

1. Find where the difficulty is first stated. It often comes as a rhetorical question, a
   counterexample or an everyday way of speaking.
2. Follow where the concepts appear: in which paragraph does the author first use the key term,
   and what word stood in its place before? The moment the wording changes is where a choice was
   made.
3. Find the load-bearing point: which passage, if it were removed, would leave the later
   conclusions without support? Read that passage in full.
4. Watch for concessions and turns: the words with which the author grants something and moves on
   ("however", "even so", "granted that", and their equivalents in the text's language). Where
   does the author admit the other side's strength, and how is the concession turned into the
   author's own advance?

## What to write

Write in the order difficulty, choice, advance, but not as a three-line outline:

- **Difficulty**: whose difficulty, in what situation. Quote the words and give the place.
- **Choice**: which concept, distinction or example the author introduces, and what it adds to the
  earlier way of putting things.
- **Cost**: which questions the choice makes harder to answer, and whether the author deals with
  them.
- **Advance**: where the argument finally lands, and in what it is stronger and weaker than the
  opening claim.
- **Your reconstruction apart from the author's words**: what the author states, what you infer
  from the structure, and the doubts you raise go in different sentences.

## Take care

- Do not pass over a point with "the author holds that …". Be able to say in which paragraph the
  author holds it.
- Do not complete an argument the author did not write. Where there is a jump, write "from A to B a
  step is missing" and say which step.
- A reconstruction yields an explanation, not a summary; a good one sends the reader back to a
  particular place in the text.
