# Kinds of question, levels, paradigms

Maps of fields say where a question lives; these say what kind of question it is, at which
level it is asked, and through which lens. Every entry is somebody's scheme, transcribed from
the source named under it; use it as a prompt for what the plan has not asked, not as a form
to fill. `(editorial)` marks our own synthesis.

## 1. Kinds of question

### Tinbergen's four questions (1963)

Tinbergen's "On aims and methods of ethology" (*Z. Tierpsychol.* 20: 410–433) names four
"major problems of biology" for any trait: **causation** (mechanism: how does it work?),
**ontogeny** (development: how did it develop in the individual?), **survival value**
(function, "current utility": what is it for?) and **evolution** (phylogeny: how did it
evolve in the lineage?). Two groupings are standard. Mayr's: *proximate* = causation and
ontogeny, the "how" questions; *ultimate* = function and evolution, the "why" questions.
Tinbergen's own: *current* = mechanism and function; *historical* = development and
evolution. Bateson and Laland's fiftieth-anniversary review finds the scheme intact, the four
mostly studied apart, and very few characters for which all four have been answered.
(editorial) Carried into the humanities and social sciences: mechanism = how does it work
now; origin = how did it come to be, in this case; function = what does it do, for whom;
history = how did it change over the long run. A plan that asks only one of the four has three
blind spots; a plan that answers a "why" with a "how" has changed the question.
Source: https://www.cell.com/trends/ecology-evolution/abstract/S0169-5347(13)00236-X
(Bateson & Laland 2013, *Trends Ecol. Evol.* 28: 712–718; text at
https://lalandlab.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/files/2015/08/Publication199.pdf) (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Aristotle's four causes

In *Physics* II 3 and *Metaphysics* V 2 Aristotle distinguishes four explanatory roles, each
the answer to a different question: the **material** cause ("what is it made out of?"), the
**formal** cause ("what is it?", the essence), the **efficient** cause ("where does the
change come from?") and the **final** cause ("what is its good?", that for the sake of which).
Knowledge of a thing is knowledge of its why, and an adequate explanation may need all four.
For a plan: which of the four does each task supply, and has anyone asked what the object is
*made of* (sources, materials, people, money) as opposed to what it is *for*?
Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-causality/ (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Descriptive, explanatory, interpretive, normative, evaluative

Research-design texts group research questions into **what** (description: what is the case,
how much, what pattern), **why** (explanation: what produced it) and **how** (intervention:
how could it be changed); Blaikie and Priest treat the three as the order of a research
process. Dilthey's contrast between the *explanation* of outer experience and the
*understanding* of lived experience adds the **interpretive** question: what did it mean to
those who did it, and how is that meaning to be read? Value theory separates the
**evaluative** (good, bad, better, for whom) from the **deontic** or normative (right, ought,
permitted, just). (editorial) Five prompts, then: what is the case; why; what did it mean to
them; is it good, by whose standard; what ought to be done. A plan that shows something is bad
and concludes what ought to be done has a gap where an argument should be.
Sources: https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Designing+Social+Research%3A+The+Logic+of+Anticipation%2C+3rd+Edition-p-9781509517411
(Blaikie & Priest, Polity 2019); https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hermeneutics/ ;
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/value-theory/ (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Comparative and counterfactual questions

Skocpol and Somers distinguish three logics of comparative history: **parallel demonstration
of theory** (cases shown one after another to fit a theory), **contrast of contexts** (cases
juxtaposed to bring out each one's particularity) and **macro-causal analysis** (cases used as
an approximation to controlled comparison, in the manner of Mill's methods). Each answers a
different question; a plan should say which one it means by "compare". Counterfactuals ("if A
had not happened, would B?") sit under most causal claims — the Lewis–Stalnaker semantics
evaluates them at the closest worlds where the antecedent holds — but they are
context-sensitive (what is held fixed) and non-monotonic (adding detail to the antecedent can
flip the answer). For every causal claim in a plan, ask what its counterfactual is, what is
held fixed, and whether a comparison case can stand in for it.
Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/uses-of-comparative-history-in-macrosocial-inquiry/CD5E420879461CBCE03A87E1AA5E84A4
(Skocpol & Somers 1980, *CSSH* 22: 174–197); https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/counterfactuals/
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### "What would falsify this?" (Popper)

Popper's demarcation: a claim is scientific when it is incompatible with some possible
observation, and "corroboration" counts only as the positive result of a genuinely risky
prediction that might have failed. In logic one genuine counter-instance refutes a universal
claim; in method the decision to accept a falsification is hard (measurement error, auxiliary
hypotheses), and Popper allowed that theories are rescued by modifying auxiliaries —
legitimately, unless the modification is *ad hoc*. Lakatos's objection (there are no critical
tests; a "protective belt" absorbs anomalies) is the standing caveat. As a planning question:
for each conclusion the plan hopes to reach, write the finding that would overturn it and the
task that could produce that finding. If no task could, the conclusion is not being tested.
Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/ (accessed 2026-08-25)

## 2. Levels of analysis

### Singer's level-of-analysis problem (1961)

Singer (*World Politics* 14(1): 77–92) argues that every inquiry chooses between the parts
and the whole, "the trees or the forest", and that in international relations the two usual
candidates — the international **system** and the **national state** — each buy something:
the systemic level gives comprehensiveness and patterns of interaction at the cost of
overstating the system's grip on its actors and homogenising them; the state level gives
differentiation, goals and decision processes at the cost of parochialism and harder
comparison. The choice shapes description, explanation and prediction, must be made
explicitly, and cannot be a matter of habit. For a plan: at which level is each claim made, at
which level does its evidence sit, and does any task move between levels without saying so?
Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/levelofanalysis-problem-in-international-relations/F2442CC11B7229283040B665AE050779
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Micro, meso, macro (sociology)

Introductory sociology describes three levels. **Micro**: face-to-face interaction among
specific individuals in specific settings. **Meso**: the properties of networks, groups and
organisations — a school, a firm, a team, a local public — where many roles are coordinated
at once and the question is how collectivities constrain or enable their members. **Macro**:
institutions, classes, whole societies, whose properties are described by statistics,
cross-cultural comparison or history and which Durkheim treated as sui generis. The meso level
is the one most often missing from plans: individual stories and national aggregates are easy
to find, the organisation in between is not.
Source: https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/introductiontosociology3/chapter/7-1-how-is-society-possible/
(*Introduction to Sociology*, 3rd Canadian ed., 7.1) (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Coleman's boat (macro–micro–macro)

Coleman's diagram (introduced 1986; central to *Foundations of Social Theory*, 1990) draws a
macro-level explanation as a detour through individuals: a macro condition shapes
individuals' situations (macro→micro), individuals act (micro→micro), and their actions
combine into a macro outcome (micro→macro). Hedström and Swedberg's 1998 version names the
three legs situational, action-formation and transformational mechanisms. Ylikoski reads the
diagram as a heuristic for what a mechanism-based explanation must supply, not a metaphysical
claim; the hardest leg is usually the last, the aggregation. For a plan: if a task explains a
macro pattern by a macro cause, the boat asks what people did in between; if it explains one by
individual motives, the boat asks how the motives added up.
Sources: https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/171684239/Understanding_the_Coleman_boat_2021.pdf
(Ylikoski 2021); https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.soc.012809.102632
(Hedström & Ylikoski 2010, *Annu. Rev. Sociol.* 36: 49–67) (accessed 2026-08-25)

## 3. Argument structure

### Toulmin's six elements

*The Uses of Argument* (1958; updated ed. 2003, ch. III "The Layout of Arguments") lays an
argument out as **claim** (what is asserted), **data** or **grounds** (the evidence offered),
**warrant** (the general rule that licenses the step from grounds to claim), **backing** (what
supports the warrant itself), **qualifier** (the strength claimed: "presumably", "in most
cases") and **rebuttal** (the conditions under which the warrant does not hold). The red
team's ruler: for each conclusion in the plan, which of the six has no task behind it?
Warrants and backing are the usual gaps — plans collect grounds and forget to ask why those
grounds bear on the claim.
Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/uses-of-argument/26CF801BC12004587B66778297D5567C ;
six-part summary https://itservicedesk.sjsu.edu/writingcenter/docs/handouts/Toulmin%20Model%20of%20Argumentative%20Writing.pdf
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Walton's argumentation schemes

Walton, Reed and Macagno (*Argumentation Schemes*, Cambridge 2008) catalogue 96 recurring,
defeasible patterns of reasoning, each paired with critical questions an opponent may put.
The ones met most often, following the book's chapters and the SEP survey: argument from
**position to know** and from **expert opinion** (is the source credible, in this domain, and
is this what it said?); from **analogy**, **classification** and **precedent**; **practical
reasoning** (from goal to action); from **lack of evidence**; from **consequences**, **fear or
danger**, **slippery slope**; from **popular opinion**, **commitment** and **character** (ad
hominem in its legitimate and fallacious forms); **causal** schemes (cause to effect, effect
to cause, correlation to cause); argument from **sign** and from **example**. For a plan: name
the scheme each task's expected conclusion will rest on and put its critical questions into
the task — they are a ready-made list of what to check.
Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/argumentation-schemes/9AE7E4E6ABDE690565442B2BD516A8B6
(chapter list: https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/23749/frontmatter/9780521723749_frontmatter.pdf);
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-informal/ (accessed 2026-08-25)

## 4. Cognitive levels and 5W1H

### Bloom's revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl 2001)

The revision keeps six categories of cognitive process, now as verbs — **Remember**,
**Understand**, **Apply**, **Analyze**, **Evaluate**, **Create** (the top two swap the
original order of Evaluation and Synthesis) — and crosses them with a knowledge dimension of
four kinds: **factual**, **conceptual**, **procedural**, **metacognitive**. For a plan: most
tasks sit at Remember and Understand (find, summarise); the question is which tasks require
Analyze (take apart, relate), Evaluate (judge against stated criteria) and Create (a synthesis
that did not exist), and whether the plan expects a conclusion at a level no task reaches.
Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2 (Krathwohl 2002,
*Theory Into Practice* 41(4): 212–218; text at
https://ocw.metu.edu.tr/pluginfile.php/9009/mod_resource/content/1/s15430421tip4104_2.pdf)
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### 5W1H

Who, what, when, where, why, how: the journalist's checklist for a lead, taught in that form
since the 1910s and memorialised in Kipling's "six honest serving-men" (*Just So Stories*,
1902); the same list descends from the classical "circumstances" of an act (Aristotle's
*Ethics*, Hermagoras, Cicero, Boethius) used to particularise a case in rhetoric and law.
Cheap, and worth running on every task: it catches the missing *when* (period) and *where*
(place) that the facets file also asks for.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ws (accessed 2026-08-25)

## 5. Communication: Lasswell's formula (1948)

Lasswell's wording, in "The Structure and Function of Communication in Society" (in Bryson,
ed., *The Communication of Ideas*, 1948): "Who / Says What / In Which Channel / To Whom /
With What Effect?" Each question names a branch of research — control analysis, content
analysis, media analysis, audience analysis, effect analysis — and the essay adds three
functions of communication in society: surveillance of the environment, correlation of the
parts of society in responding to it, and transmission of the social heritage. For anything
textual in the plan: a task on "what the source says" has answered one question of five; who
produced it, through which channel, for which audience and with what effect are four more, and
the last is the hardest to evidence.
Source: https://pracownik.kul.pl/files/37108/public/Lasswell.pdf (text of the 1948 essay);
context https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasswell%27s_model_of_communication (accessed 2026-08-25)

## 6. Paradigms: which lens has not been tried

Each list is transcribed from one named survey; a school absent from that survey is not
absent from the field.

### History

Green and Troup, *The Houses of History* (2nd ed., Manchester UP 2016), chapters 2–15: the
empiricists · Marxist historians · psychoanalysis and history · the Annales · historical
sociology · quantitative history · anthropology and ethnohistorians · the question of
narrative · gender and history · the challenge of poststructuralism/postmodernism ·
postcolonial perspectives · public history · oral history · history of emotions. (editorial)
Standard but not in that list: global and transnational history, microhistory, environmental
history, digital history, history of science and knowledge. For a plan: a political-narrative
plan has not asked the Annales question (long-run structures and conjunctures), the
quantitative question (how many, how distributed), the gender question (whose labour, whose
body) or the oral and public question (who remembers it, and how).
Source: https://manchesteruniversitypress.co.uk/9780719096211/ (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Sociology

Burrell and Morgan (*Sociological Paradigms and Organisational Analysis*, 1979) cross two
dimensions — assumptions about social science (subjective–objective) and about society
(regulation–radical change) — into four paradigms: **functionalist**, **interpretive**,
**radical humanist**, **radical structuralist**. Ritzer (*The American Sociologist* 10(3):
156–167, 1975) finds three paradigms, each defined by exemplar, image of the subject matter,
theories and methods: **social facts** (Durkheim; structural-functional and conflict theory;
surveys and the historical-comparative method), **social definition** (Weber; action theory,
symbolic interactionism, phenomenology and ethnomethodology; observation), **social
behaviour** (Skinner; behavioural and exchange theory; experiment). (editorial) Later
families to add: rational choice, structuration, practice theory, network and field theory,
analytical sociology.
Sources: https://www.routledge.com/Sociological-Paradigms-and-Organisational-Analysis-Elements-of-the-Sociology-of-Corporate-Life/Burrell-Morgan/p/book/9781857421149 ;
https://www.jstor.org/stable/27702185 ; Ritzer's own summary https://doi.org/10.17161/str.1808.4844
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Literary theory

Eagleton, *Literary Theory: An Introduction* (anniversary ed., 2008), chapters: the rise of
English (liberal humanism, practical criticism, New Criticism) · phenomenology, hermeneutics,
reception theory · structuralism and semiotics · post-structuralism · psychoanalysis ·
conclusion: political criticism. Tyson, *Critical Theory Today* (4th ed., Routledge),
chapters: psychoanalytic · Marxist · feminist · New Criticism · reader-response ·
structuralist · deconstructive · new historical and cultural · lesbian, gay and queer ·
African American · postcolonial · ecocriticism. (editorial) Add: book history and reception
studies, digital and distant reading, world literature. For a plan: a "what the text means"
task assumes one of these; the reception question (who read it, how, with what effect) is the
one most often unasked.
Sources: https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/ecip0811/2008006964.html ;
https://www.routledge.com/Critical-Theory-Today-A-User-Friendly-Guide/Tyson/p/book/9780367709426
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Philosophy

Method families, each with an SEP entry. **Conceptual analysis**: decompositional,
regressive, transformative and connective conceptions of analysis. **Phenomenology**: the
structures of experience as lived from the first-person point of view (Husserl, Heidegger,
Merleau-Ponty). **Hermeneutics**: interpretation and the hermeneutic circle (Schleiermacher,
Dilthey, Heidegger, Gadamer). **Pragmatism**: meaning and truth through consequences for
practice (Peirce, James, Dewey; revived by Rorty). **Experimental philosophy**: testing
philosophical assumptions, including case intuitions, with empirical methods. **History of
ideas**: Skinner's argument that a text is understood only through what its author was doing
in the linguistic conventions of the time (the "Cambridge School"). **Comparative
philosophy**: bringing traditions to bear on each other's problems, with the standing danger
of assimilating one to the other's frameworks.
Sources: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/analysis/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/phenomenology/ ;
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hermeneutics/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/ ;
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/experimental-philosophy/ ; https://www.jstor.org/stable/2504188
(Skinner 1969, *History and Theory* 8: 3–53); https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/comparphil-chiwes/
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Law

Watkins and Burton (eds.), *Research Methods in Law* (2nd ed., Routledge 2018), chapters:
**doctrinal** research (Hutchinson: verifying the authority and status of the doctrine is the
first step of any legal project) · **socio-legal** studies (Cownie and Bradney: an inclusive
label covering law in context, empirical and critical work) · empirical research · legal
research and the humanities · **legal history** · **comparative law** and its methodology
(Samuel: comparing two countries' rules is not in itself a method) · **critical legal** method
as attitude (Minkkinen) · **economic analysis** of law · feminist approaches · law and
anthropology. Economic analysis: Posner's positive claim (common-law rules are efficient) and
normative claim (they ought to be). Critical legal studies: from the 1977 Wisconsin
conference; law as bound up with social power, drawing on legal realism and European social
theory. Comparative law: the functional method (Zweigert) is the mainstream; the alternatives
are comparative legal history, legal transplants and comparative legal cultures.
Sources: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781315386652_A30449886/preview-9781315386652_A30449886.pdf
(Watkins & Burton, contents and introduction); https://ojs.deakin.edu.au/index.php/dlr/article/view/70
(Hutchinson & Duncan 2012); https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/legal-econanalysis/ ;
https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/critical_legal_theory ;
https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:a7e93134-bd18-47e8-8e37-c2526e03bbc9/Michaels-oxfordhb-9780199296064-e-011.pdf
(Michaels, *Oxford Handbook of Comparative Law*, 2006) (accessed 2026-08-25)

### Anthropology

Barnard, *History and Theory in Anthropology* (2nd ed., Cambridge UP 2021), chapters 3–13:
changing perspectives on **evolution** · **diffusionist** and culture-area theories ·
**functionalism** and **structural-functionalism** · action and process · **Marxist**
perspectives · from relativism to cognitive science · **structuralism** · poststructuralists
and feminists · mavericks · **interpretive** approaches · postmodernism and its aftermath.
McGee and Warms, *Anthropological Theory* (5th ed., McGraw-Hill 2012), adds as units:
historical particularism (Boas) · culture and personality · neomaterialism (evolutionary,
functionalist, ecological, Marxist — including Harris's **cultural materialism**) ·
ethnoscience and cognitive anthropology · sociobiology and behavioural ecology · gender ·
**symbolic and interpretive** anthropology (Douglas, Turner, Geertz) · Bourdieu and Foucault
as background to postmodernism · globalisation, power and agency. **Practice theory** is
Ortner's name (1984) for the post-1970s turn to Bourdieu, Giddens and Sahlins: how practice
reproduces and changes structure. The **ontological turn** (Holbraad and Pedersen, Cambridge
2017) takes ethnographic material as a source of concepts rather than of beliefs about a
shared world (Wagner, Strathern, Viveiros de Castro).
Sources: https://assets.cambridge.org/97811088/37958/frontmatter/9781108837958_frontmatter.pdf ;
https://content.ub.hu-berlin.de/monographs/toc/ethnologie/BV039646542.pdf ;
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/abs/theory-in-anthropology-since-the-sixties/E1A11A2339C17170C6BAB501824DF010
(Ortner 1984, *CSSH* 26: 126–166); https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/ontological-turn/2BAEBEAAAA3340EFC7ABFB750206EED0
(accessed 2026-08-25)

### Political science

Lowndes, Marsh and Stoker (eds.), *Theory and Methods in Political Science* (4th ed.,
Palgrave 2017), part I: **behaviouralism** · **rational choice** · **institutionalism** ·
**constructivism and interpretive theory** · feminist and gender approaches · Marxism ·
poststructuralism · political psychology · normative theory. Hall and Taylor (*Political
Studies* 44(5): 936–957, 1996) split the new institutionalism into three — **historical**,
**rational-choice** and **sociological** — which differ on how institutions shape behaviour
(a calculus approach against a cultural one) and on how institutions arise and change. For a
plan on any political object: which of these has the plan assumed, and what would the
rational-choice reading say that the interpretive one cannot, or the reverse?
Sources: https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/theory-and-methods-in-political-science-9781137603517/ ;
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9248.1996.tb00343.x (text:
https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/hall/files/political_studies_1996.pdf) (accessed 2026-08-25)
